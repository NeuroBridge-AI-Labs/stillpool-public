## A link is not proof

<Simple>
A citation is a note in an answer that points to a source, such as a study. It gives the authors, the year and where it was published.

A chatbot is a program that can write text for you, such as ChatGPT. Its answers can be factually wrong.

A chatbot can write a citation that looks right and leads nowhere. It can also name a real paper that says something else.

So the check is not reading the citation. The check is opening it. Lesson 1.6, Why they make things up, looks at why chatbots can do this.
</Simple>

<Deep>
Two researchers, Walters and Wilder, tested this in April 2023. ChatGPT is a chatbot. They asked two versions of it to write short papers on 42 topics. They found 636 citations in the 84 papers, and searched databases and websites for each one.

They wrote that 55% (just over 1 in 2) of the GPT-3.5 citations and 18% (about 1 in 5) of the GPT-4 citations were fabricated. Fabricated means the work does not exist: it was never published, presented or posted. Of the real citations, 43% (about 4 in 10) from GPT-3.5 and 24% (about 1 in 4) from GPT-4 had substantive errors, such as a wrong author, title, date, journal or page number.

These are results for two models, one prompt and 42 topics, on texts made in the first week of April 2023. They are not a rate for any chatbot today. Our own test in this lesson is a separate, smaller one.
</Deep>

## Pick the claims that matter

<Simple>
A claim is one thing an answer says is true. One answer can hold many claims, and one source may back only one of them.

Here is an invented example. An answer says: "The scheme began in 2023, cut costs by a third and is open to everyone in Ireland." That is four claims. It began in 2023. It cut costs by a third. It is open to everyone. It is in Ireland.

Our steps: start with the claims that would change what you do. Open the source for each one. Find the claim in it.
</Simple>

<Deep>
A source can back the main idea of an answer and not a detail. In our test, Claude described Singer and Alexander's 2017 review in three of its answers. Two of them said paper "tends to do better for longer or more complex texts". The review's abstract says only that the medium "plays an influential role under certain text or task conditions or for certain readers". We did not open the full text, so we could not settle that detail. We left it out of our count and say so here.
</Deep>

## Open the link and compare

<Simple>
Many citations come with a DOI. A DOI is a long-lasting number that identifies a work. It is made of a prefix and a suffix, split by a slash, such as 10.1000/182.

Put https://doi.org/ in front of a DOI and it opens as a link. Then compare what opens with the citation. Check the title, the authors, the journal and the year.

If the link fails, search the exact title on Crossref. Crossref is a website where you can search the details of published papers, books and more. If you still find nothing, write "could not find it". Do not write "it does not exist".
</Simple>

<Deep>
The doi.org page says a DOI can identify "any object". So a DOI opening a record does not tell you the record is the work the answer named. In our test, ten of Ministral's DOIs opened a different work from the one named.

Here is how we checked each of the forty sources, in this order. First, we opened the link as given. Second, if it was a DOI, we asked the DOI resolver, the doi.org service that sends you to the work a DOI names, and the Crossref record what it opens. Third, if it failed, we searched the exact title on Crossref and then on the web. We used no university library search, because we have no library login. Some publishers' sites refused our requests, and we say so in the notes.

A source is real and relevant when it has the named title and authors, the DOI matches, and its own summary backs the answer's main finding. It is real but unrelated when the DOI opens another work, or the work does not say what the answer says. It is not there when no work of that title could be found.

A search that finds nothing cannot prove a paper never existed. That is why our word is "not there", and why our note says "we could not find it".
</Deep>

The choices are real and relevant, real but unrelated, not there, or can't tell yet. Real but unrelated means the link or the details lead to a different work. Not there means we could not find it.

Then you see what we found and how we checked. Nothing is counted or saved.

After the four, you can read all ten answers in full, with the model and the date.
</Simple>

</Deep>

## What our ten answers showed

<Simple>
We asked two AI models, Claude Sonnet 5.5 and Ministral 3 14B, the same question five times each. It was about reading on paper or screen. It asked for four sources with links. We recorded the answers on 8 October 2026. We checked every source on 9 October 2026.

Claude Sonnet 5.5 named four sources in each of its five answers, twenty in all. All twenty were real and relevant. They were five papers, each named several times.

Ministral 3 14B also named twenty. None was real and relevant. Twelve were real but unrelated: the link or the details led to a different work. Eight were not there: we could not find them.

Together, the forty sources were twenty real and relevant, twelve real but unrelated and eight not there.
</Simple>

<Deep>
Claude's five answers each ended by asking the reader to check. The fifth said: "I'm giving DOI links from memory, so please verify them." None of Ministral's five answers asked for a check. Its answers gave authors, a journal, volume, pages and a link, so they looked complete.

Real does not mean right. In three of Claude's answers, the description of Kong, Seo and Zhai (2018) said the size of the paper advantage varied with factors such as text type. The paper's abstract says: "None of the moderators were significant at the 0.05 level." The moderators are the factors the paper tested. We counted the paper as real and relevant, because its main finding matches. We marked the answer's description as going past the paper.

A real title can sit in a wrong citation. One Ministral citation was "Clancy et al. (2014), The pen is mightier than the keyboard". Its DOI opens a paper of that title by Mueller and Oppenheimer, and its subtitle is "Advantages of Longhand Over Laptop Note Taking". Another was a Scientific American piece called "The Reading Brain in the Digital Age", credited to "Clancy 2015". A piece of that title by Ferris Jabr was published on 11 April 2013. The answer says paper may activate brain regions such as the "hippocampus for memory". The piece's text does not contain the word "hippocampus".

This is two models, one question and one day. It does not show how often AI invents citations. Walters and Wilder's study above is the sourced measure for two earlier models, and it too says only what it tested.
</Deep>

## Look beyond the page

<Simple>
Sometimes you must judge a website, not a paper. Lateral reading means leaving the page and opening other sources to judge it.

A 2019 study by Wineburg and McGrew watched 45 people judge websites. Ten were professional fact checkers, who check claims as a job. The fact checkers left a site after a quick look and opened new tabs to judge it.

They reached better-supported conclusions, and in much less time. Our steps for an answer work the same way: open the source, then look elsewhere.
</Simple>

<Deep>
Wineburg and McGrew (2019) sampled 45 experienced internet users: 10 PhD historians, 10 professional fact checkers and 25 Stanford University undergraduates. They thought aloud as they judged live websites on issues such as bullying, minimum wage and teacher tenure. Historians and students "read vertically, staying within a website to evaluate its reliability". Fact checkers "read laterally, leaving a site after a quick scan and opening up new browser tabs".

The abstract says the fact checkers "arrived at more warranted conclusions in a fraction of the time". The study is about web pages, not chatbot answers. We apply the idea to an answer as our own step. The study does not test it.
</Deep>

## When to ask a qualified person

<Simple>
Checking a source is not enough for some topics. For health, law or money, ask a qualified person. That means someone trained and allowed to give that kind of advice.

This lesson does not teach those fields. An AI answer can help you plan questions to ask. It should not be the last word.
</Simple>

<Deep>
Some of these people sit on public registers. The Medical Council's page says: "By law all doctors have to register with the Medical Council in order to practise", and that you can check a doctor is registered. The Central Bank of Ireland's Registers site lists the financial firms the Bank regulates, and says to contact the Bank if you are unsure of a firm's authorised status. The Law Society of Ireland describes itself as a body for Irish solicitors.

This is our advice, not a rule from any of these bodies. We checked these three pages on 9 October 2026. We could not open some other pages we tried.
</Deep>
