## Pictures and sounds are numbers

<Simple>
A person can name a doodle of a cat. How does a computer put a name to it?

In this lesson, to recognise means this. A model is a program that has learned patterns from examples. A model names what a picture shows, or writes out the words in a recording. It does this from patterns learned from examples.

To a computer, a picture is a grid of numbers. Each small square is a pixel, and its number says how dark it is. In this lesson each doodle is drawn on a grid of 28 by 28 squares.

Sound becomes numbers too. OpenAI's Whisper (paper, December 2022) is a program that writes out speech. It first turns every recording into 16,000 numbers for each second of sound.
</Simple>

<Deep>
This lesson looks at these beliefs: "It is equally capable at everything" and "It is neutral and unbiased". Each is set against dated evidence below.

Each doodle becomes 784 numbers, from 0 for a blank square to 1 for a full one. Google also publishes every doodle drawn on a grid of the same size. Its README says: "All the simplified drawings have been rendered into a 28x28 grayscale bitmap in numpy .npy format." Our drawing rule follows the settings Google gave for that version.

Lesson 1.2 compares written rules with learning from examples. Lesson 1.7 shows how training works. Neither is needed first.
</Deep>

## Learning from labelled examples

<Simple>
A model learns from examples that people have labelled. Each example is a picture or a recording, with its label. The label is the answer the model should learn to give.

Google released the Quick, Draw! dataset in August 2017. A dataset is a collection of examples, here drawings. Its README, checked in September 2026, lists 50 million drawings of 345 kinds. A README is the page that describes a dataset.

Players of its online game Quick, Draw! drew them. Each drawing's label is the word the player was asked to draw. Some drawings are incomplete or do not match the prompt.

People choose the examples and the labels, and so what a model can learn. For this lesson, we chose six kinds and 2,000 doodles of each for training. We kept only doodles the game had recognised.
</Simple>

<Deep>
Google's glossary defines a label as "the "answer" or "result" portion of an example", and a labeled example as "An example that contains one or more features and a label."

Google says over 15 million players took part (its data page, checked in September 2026). Its README says the dataset was "contributed by players of the game Quick, Draw!". Each drawing is "tagged with metadata including what the player was asked to draw".

The OECD and European Union's framework for AI literacy, the skills and knowledge to use AI in an informed way, says AI systems are "trained to identify patterns among data elements that humans have selected, categorised and prioritised".
</Deep>

## A chance for each kind

<Simple>
A model that names the kind of thing it sees is called a classifier. It gives each kind it learned a chance.

The chances add up to 1 (100 in 100). Its guess is the kind with the biggest chance. In this lesson the chances are shown as words, like "N in 100".
</Simple>

<Deep>
Naming what is in a picture is called image recognition, or image classification. The model in this lesson is one of the simplest kinds, multinomial logistic regression.

For each of its kinds, it keeps one number, a weight, for each of the 784 squares, plus one more number. To guess, it multiplies each square's darkness by that kind's weight for the square. It adds them up with the extra number, and turns the six totals into chances.

The model has 4,710 numbers (6 kinds times 785). The copy has 2,355 numbers. Google's glossary says convolutional neural networks, a kind of neural network (a model built from layers of numbers), "have had great success in certain kinds of problems, such as image recognition."

Our training used 300 rounds of gradient descent on the labelled doodles. Each round changed the numbers a little so the guesses got less wrong. Lesson 1.7 covers training.
</Deep>

<Simple>
Pick a doodle, or draw your own. First, guess what the model will name. The model's numbers do not change while you draw.

Then switch to a copy of the model. It was trained the same way on the same doodles, but on three kinds only: apple, house and umbrella. This shows what happens when a model meets a kind it never learned.
</Simple>

<Deep>
The model learned from 12,000 doodles, 2,000 of each of six kinds: apple, car, cat, fish, house and umbrella. We took each kind's doodles from the start of its file on Google's servers.

A model is tested on examples it did not learn from. We kept back 500 doodles of each kind, 3,000 in all. The model named the right kind for most of them, and got some wrong. A test like this says how the model does on new doodles like its own, and nothing about anything else.

The copy was tested on held-back cars, cats and fish too. Its only possible labels are apple, house and umbrella. It named one of them for every car, cat and fish, so it was wrong about all of them.
</Deep>

## Only the kinds it learned

<Simple>
A model like this can only name the kinds it learned. Shown anything else, it still names one of them. It has no other answer, not even "none of these". So it is always wrong about a kind it never learned.

It can also go wrong on new examples of a kind it did learn, when they are unlike its examples.

In 2020, researchers wrote that a cow on a beach, instead of on grass, is not named correctly.

The six-kind model named the right kind for most new doodles of its six kinds. It got some wrong. A good test result is not being good at everything. A test on new doodles of six kinds says nothing about other pictures.
</Simple>

<Deep>
Google's glossary gives an example of something out of distribution: "an image of the planet Saturn would be considered out of distribution for a dataset consisting of cat images."

The cow comes from a 2020 review of image models: "a familiar background can be as important for recognition as the object itself, and sometimes even more important". In a 2018 study of photos from camera traps, image classifiers did very well at the places they were trained on, and poorly at new places.

A good score on one test is not a good score everywhere. OpenAI's Whisper researchers wrote in December 2022 that machine learning methods "are exceedingly adept at finding patterns within a training dataset which boost performance on held-out data from the same dataset." They added: "However, some of these patterns are brittle and spurious and don’t generalize to other datasets and distributions."

The evidence for the belief. In a survey of 104 Duke University students (data collected in April 2025), almost all (94%) agreed that AI is not equally accurate in all areas of study. So few of those students can have held the belief. It is one small US survey. Lesson 4.9 will cover when not to use AI.
</Deep>

## Sound, and whose voice

<Simple>
A model can also do worse on people unlike those it learned from. Here is a study of voices.

A speech system writes out the words in a recording. The written words are a transcript.

In a study published in 2020, five speech systems made more errors for Black speakers than for white speakers. It was almost twice as many on average. The systems were tested before October 2019.

The authors said the likely cause was too little audio from Black speakers in training.

An accent is a way of speaking. The errors are the system's. Lesson 3.2 will go further.
</Simple>

<Deep>
How one speech recogniser takes sound in. Whisper turns each second of a recording into 16,000 numbers, then into a spectrogram made of slices 25 milliseconds long. A spectrogram is a chart of how loud each pitch is, moment by moment. Whisper then predicts the transcript. Its models learned from 680,000 hours of recorded speech paired with transcripts, collected from the internet.

The study, published 23 March 2020, tested five speech recognition systems, by Amazon, Apple, Google, IBM and Microsoft. They wrote out interviews with 42 white speakers and 73 Black speakers in five US cities (19.8 hours of audio). All five made more errors for the Black speakers: on average, about 35 errors for every 100 words (a word error rate of 0.35) against about 19 (0.19) for the white speakers, almost twice as many.

A word error rate measures how far a machine's transcript is from a person's transcript of the same speech. It counts errors per word, not people.

The authors traced the gap to the part of each system that handles the sound of speech, the acoustic model. It was not the part that handles words and grammar: the gap was just as large for the same phrases said by Black and white speakers. They wrote: "The likely cause of this shortcoming is insufficient audio data from black speakers when training the models." They proposed training with more varied recordings, including African American Vernacular English.

The limits. It is one US study of five systems tested before October 2019, on interviews in US English.
</Deep>

## Making pictures and voices

<Simple>
Some AI makes pictures and sounds instead of naming them. The Deep layer shows how, in outline.

Lesson 4.5 will cover telling real pictures and voices from fake ones.
</Simple>

<Deep>
Google's glossary lists image generation models and audio generation models among generative AI, which is AI that makes new content such as pictures and sound.

One design for making pictures is a diffusion model, as Ho, Jain and Abbeel described theirs in June 2020. In their design, noise is added to pictures a little at a time in training until nothing of the picture is left. The model learns to undo each step. To make a new picture, it starts from pure random noise and removes some at each step. Not every diffusion model works this way.

A picture from a written prompt: Google's Imagen (May 2022) paired a large language model, which turns the prompt's words into numbers, with diffusion models that make the picture. Its paper says it starts from pure random noise and makes it a little less noisy at each step.

A voice from written text: Google's Tacotron 2 (posted December 2017) turns text into a spectrogram. A second network, a vocoder, turns that chart into a sound wave.

Copying a voice: Microsoft researchers reported in January 2023 that their research system, VALL-E, could make speech in the voice of a person it had not trained on. It needed a recording of that person only 3 seconds long. That is their claim about their own system. Lesson 4.5 will cover telling real voices from fake ones, and lesson 3.6 who owns AI-made work.
</Deep>

## Is it neutral?

<Simple>
People choose the examples, and the examples shape what a model can do. The copy in this lesson shows it.

Bias means a system works worse for some groups of people than for others. Bias can come from training data. Lesson 3.2 will cover bias.
</Simple>

<Deep>
The OECD and European Union's framework says: "Bias inherently exists in AI systems, which can also reflect societal biases embedded in training data or algorithm design."

The belief, measured directly. In an online survey by Ipsos in 32 countries (fieldwork 20 March to 3 April 2026, published June 2026), more than half (55%), on average across the 32 countries, agreed with "I trust artificial intelligence to not discriminate or show bias towards any group of people". Just under half (47%) agreed with the same statement about people.

The limits. The figure is an average of the 32 countries' results, not of all their people. In 15 of the countries, Ireland among them, Ipsos says its samples are more urban, more educated or more affluent than the population. It is one 2026 survey.
</Deep>
