## Three studies, with authors

<Simple>
A chatbot names three studies, with authors, years and journals. Would you put them in an essay?

A journal is where researchers publish their studies. The answer looks ready to use. But a detail in it could be made up. This lesson shows why, and what to do.
</Simple>

<Deep>
This lesson looks at these beliefs: "Confident and detailed means correct", "Hallucination is basically solved now" and "Its citations are real evidence". Each is set against dated evidence below. Every study, test and case carries its date.
</Deep>

## Why it happens

<Simple>
A chatbot writes its answer one likely piece at a time (lesson 1.4). Each piece comes from chances its model learned in training.

Nothing in those steps checks the facts. So likely is not the same as true.
</Simple>

<Deep>
In September 2025, four researchers (three at OpenAI, one at Georgia Tech) argued that training itself leads to some errors. Their paper says: "we show that even if the training data were error-free, the objectives optimized during language model training would lead to errors being generated." It is their argument, with a mathematical analysis. It is not a measured rate for any chatbot.

One part of their first reason is about facts seen only once in training. A base model is the model after its first training on text, before it is tuned into a chatbot. They argue that facts that appear only once in its training text are the kind it is expected to get wrong. A famous birthday, like Einstein's, appears more than once. Others may appear only once, for example in an obituary. If a fifth (20%) of birthday facts appear exactly once, they expect a base model to get at least a fifth of birthday facts wrong. That is a minimum error rate for such facts, not a share of all errors. They also note that language models seldom get wrong the facts that are mentioned often, such as Einstein's birthday.

Their second reason is about tests. They argue that most tests of language models give a point for a right answer and nothing for "I don't know". That is like an exam with no penalty for guessing: a model that guesses scores higher than one that says it is unsure. Of ten widely used tests they reviewed, only one gave any credit for showing uncertainty. They argue this is why made-up answers survive the later training that tunes a model into a chatbot. In their words, "the training and evaluation procedures reward guessing over acknowledging uncertainty".

A dated example from the same paper: on 9 May 2025, the authors asked three popular language models for the title of one author's PhD thesis. They were GPT-4o in ChatGPT, DeepSeek R1 in the DeepSeek app, and Llama 4 Scout on Hugging Face. None gave the right title or year, and none of them searched the web.

A search tool does not remove the problem. The same paper argues that "the binary grading system itself still rewards guessing whenever search fails to yield a confident answer." Lesson 1.5 covers search, and how a searched answer can still get its source wrong. Lesson 1.7 covers training in full.
</Deep>

## A made-up detail

<Simple>
So sometimes the text is false. An answer can hold a detail that sounds real and is not. It could be a study that does not exist, a wrong year, or a name that does not belong.

People call this a hallucination. The word is borrowed from people. Researchers note it is very different from what a person goes through. Google's glossary says "confabulation" is probably a more accurate word.

A made-up detail can look just like a real one. So the look of an answer does not show which parts are true.

Anthropic makes Claude. It says even the most advanced language models, like Claude, can sometimes write text that is factually wrong. Its web page was checked in September 2026.

The International AI Safety Report 2026 came out in February 2026. It says even systems that do complex tasks well may give citations, biographies or facts that do not exist. A citation names a source, such as a study, so a reader can find it.
</Simple>

<Deep>
Google's glossary defines a hallucination as "The production of plausible-seeming but factually incorrect output by a generative AI model that purports to be making an assertion about the real world." Its entry for confabulation says: "Confabulation is probably a more technically accurate term than hallucination. However, hallucination became popular first." The paper above notes that the error "differs fundamentally from the human perceptual experience".

The OECD and European Union's framework for AI literacy, the skills and knowledge to use AI in an informed way, says generative AI "can make fact and fabrication hard to distinguish". The AI literacy training of Ireland's Higher Education Authority (HEA) speaks of outputs that are "sometimes fluent yet wrong".

Anthropic's page says: "Even the most advanced language models, like Claude, can sometimes generate text that is factually incorrect or inconsistent with the given context." That is Anthropic's statement, checked in September 2026. The International AI Safety Report 2026 says: "Even systems that excel at complex tasks may generate non-existent citations, biographies, or facts".
</Deep>

## Sounding sure is not being right

<Simple>
How sure an answer sounds is not a sign that it is right.

In a study published in January 2025, people in the US read answers from three language models. They were GPT-3.5, PaLM2 and GPT-4o. The people tried to tell right answers from wrong ones. With the models' usual explanations, they did only slightly better than guessing.
</Simple>

<Deep>
In that study, 301 people living in the US, all native English speakers recruited through Prolific, read answers with explanations written by GPT-3.5, PaLM2 and GPT-4o. They judged how likely each answer was to be right. With the models' usual explanations, the authors write, they "were only slightly better than random guessing". Longer explanations made people more confident without making them any better at it: "longer explanations increased user confidence, even when the extra length did not improve answer accuracy."

The words mattered. Explanations that began "I am not sure" made people less confident than ones that said "I am somewhat sure", whichever model wrote them. The authors then ran a simulation, not a new experiment. From experiment 2's answers, they kept only the estimates people made on explanations whose wording fitted how likely the model's own numbers said the answer was to be right. In that simulation, people's estimates told right answers from wrong ones better than with the usual explanations of experiment 1.

In the multiple-choice experiments (answers from GPT-3.5 and PaLM2), people chose the answer the model's explanation backed in most responses (82%). The models' own internal chances told right answers from wrong ones better than the people did.

The paper does not say when its experiments ran, so it is dated here by when it was published. Its first posting, in January 2024, tested only GPT-3.5 and PaLM2.

A number a chatbot gives for its own confidence is not a check either. In a test posted by OpenAI researchers in November 2024, four OpenAI models (GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, o1-preview and o1-mini) were asked for an answer and how confident they were, as a percentage. They were right less often than they said. The authors write that the models "consistently overstate their confidence".
</Deep>

## Made-up citations, and what they cost

<Simple>
Citations can be made up too.

Two researchers had ChatGPT write short reviews in the first week of April 2023. Then they checked every citation. More than half (55%) of GPT-3.5's were to works that do not exist. So were about 1 in 5 (18%) of GPT-4's.

Made-up citations can cost people. In June 2023, a US court fined two lawyers and their law firm $5,000.

They had filed papers citing court cases that ChatGPT made up. They kept standing by them after the court questioned them. The judge also found that the two lawyers acted in bad faith.
</Simple>

<Deep>
The citation study: in the first week of April 2023, two researchers had ChatGPT (GPT-3.5 and GPT-4) write 84 short literature reviews on 42 topics. They checked all 636 works cited. More than half (55%) of GPT-3.5's citations and about 1 in 5 (18%) of GPT-4's were to works that do not exist. Many citations to real works had errors too: about 4 in 10 (43%) for GPT-3.5 and about 1 in 4 (24%) for GPT-4. It was published on 7 September 2023. These are 2023 models, and newer ones were not tested.

The court case is Mata v. Avianca, in a New York federal court. The lawyers filed papers on 1 March 2023 citing court decisions that ChatGPT had made up, with fake quotes. They kept standing by them after the court questioned them. The judge found "bad faith on the part of the individual Respondents based upon acts of conscious avoidance and false and misleading statements to the Court." On 22 June 2023 the judge fined the two lawyers and their law firm $5,000. The judge also ordered them to write to each judge falsely named as the author of a fake decision.

The judge also wrote: "there is nothing inherently improper about using a reliable artificial intelligence tool for assistance. But existing rules impose a gatekeeping role on attorneys to ensure the accuracy of their filings." The duty to check what is filed stays with the lawyers.

In Ireland, the High Court's practice direction HC 142, on generative AI in court documents, is dated 29 July 2026 by the President of the High Court. It is in operation from 1 September 2026, for civil proceedings. Among the known risks of AI tools, it lists "fictitious cases" and "academic references". It says people using AI tools to prepare submissions "must independently verify all legal authorities, citations, quotations and references relied upon in Court Documents using authoritative sources." That is a rule for court documents in Ireland's High Court.

The HEA's AI literacy training, written for staff, asks for "A curated repository of real cases, including hallucinated facts, fabricated citations, and confidently incorrect analyses". The cases in this lesson follow that idea.
</Deep>

## Is it solved?

<Simple>
Do newer systems still make things up? An international report from February 2026 says many kinds of failures, such as hallucinations, have generally become less likely.

But it says systems still often make mistakes on more complex tasks. Less often is not never.
</Simple>

<Deep>
The International AI Safety Report 2026 says: "Since the publication of the previous Report (January 2025), AI systems have generally become more reliable". It goes on: "Many kinds of failures, such as hallucinations, have generally become less likely, but systems still commonly make mistakes when performing more complex tasks."

Newer is not always fewer. In its system card for o3 and o4-mini, dated 16 April 2025, OpenAI reported its own test, PersonQA: questions about public facts about people, built to draw out made-up answers. OpenAI's hallucination rate on it was 0.33 (about 1 in 3) for o3 and 0.48 (almost half) for o4-mini, against 0.16 (about 1 in 6) for the older o1. On its other test, SimpleQA, the rates were 0.51 (about half) for o3, 0.79 (about 4 in 5) for o4-mini and 0.44 (more than 4 in 10) for o1.

OpenAI says o3 makes more claims overall, right and wrong. It calls this effect minor on SimpleQA and more pronounced on PersonQA, and says: "More research is needed to understand the cause of these results." These are one company's tests of its own models, on one date. They are not a rate for AI in general, and they do not show that newer models make more mistakes.

What students say. HEPI surveyed UK full-time undergraduates through Savanta: 1,041 in December 2024 (published February 2025) and 1,054 in December 2025 (published March 2026). It asked: "Which of the below, if any, are reasons which make you less likely to use AI tools for your studies?" About half (51%) picked "Getting false results / hallucinations" in the first survey, and about a third (35%) in the second. HEPI marks the drop as significant. The responses were weighted on demographics such as gender, institution type and year of study.

Over the same two surveys, students using AI in at least one way went from 92% to 95% (almost all). This counts a reason students give for using AI less. It does not measure how often AI is wrong, and it does not show that students think the problem is solved.
</Deep>

## What helps

<Simple>
Check any detail that matters against a source you can open yourself, such as the study itself.

Asking the same chatbot is not a check. In a US court case in 2023, a lawyer asked ChatGPT whether its cases were real. It said they were.

Lesson 4.1 will show how to check an answer's claims and citations against real sources.
</Simple>

<Deep>
In the Mata case, the lawyer asked ChatGPT "Is Varghese a real case" and "Are the other cases you provided fake". ChatGPT replied that they were real and could be found through Westlaw, LexisNexis and the Federal Reporter.

Ireland's High Court, for court documents, warns that AI outputs "may incorrectly assert, when asked, that information is accurate when it is not" (paragraph 10). Paragraph 13 says: "It is not sufficient to use the same or other GenAI Tools to confirm that information is accurate." GenAI means AI that makes new text or pictures. Its next sentence allows one route: "However, persons responsible for a Court Document may verify GenAI content by referring to original source material housed within a GenAI Tool." That is a rule for court documents in Ireland's High Court.

The OECD and European Union framework's basic level says: "Learners understand why AI-generated outputs should be verified and recognise how they can verify the content themselves."

Anthropic's guide for developers suggests one way to cut made-up answers from its own model: "Explicitly give Claude permission to admit uncertainty." That is Anthropic's advice about Claude, checked in September 2026, not a guarantee. Lesson 2.6 will cover prompting, and why asking again is not checking.

The limits. The people study is from the US, and the student surveys are from the UK. The citation study and the court case are from 2023 and involve 2023 models. OpenAI's figures are its own tests of its own models. The paper on why it happens is an argument. These studies do not report results for neurodivergent learners. The Irish High Court direction applies to court documents, not to every chatbot answer.
</Deep>
